The drawing holds these in one place. The scheme is defined once at the top of this file as named values — L1, L2, hot, neutral, PE — and every conductor on every sheet refers to what it is, never to a colour. Changing the scheme is five edits in that block and nothing anywhere else, which is the only way a drawing and a panel stay agreeing with each other.
Two things fall out of this that are worth having on the page.

The 2 × 8 block's own poles are red and blue, so the wire colour and the block agree and nothing has to be written on the panel to say which row is which. That was an open item until this scheme was chosen.

And colour now tells you what the paddle kills. Since the 12 V supply moved to the 110 V side, the voltage split is the switched split: blue or red dies, black or white lives. One glance at a conductor says whether an E-stop takes it away.
14 AWG everywhere inside is a choice, and it is generous. The conductor has to be rated for its breaker rather than for its load, and at 6 A a lighter gauge would be legitimate on three of the four branches. One wire size across the panel is worth more than the copper saved. 16 AWG is also legitimate for the run out to the remote paddle box if a neater cable is wanted there — it is behind the 1 A fuse and it is in range for both the spade tabs and the coil terminals, which take 1.5–2.5 mm².

Only one connection on these five sheets is unknown, and it is marked where it falls. The Huanyang manual gives FA/FB/FC as “N/O or N/C” without saying which leg is which, so which of FB and FC is the normally-open one is a meter check on the real drive, with the drive unpowered. Everything else carries a terminal designation off a datasheet or a listing.

Fig. 1 — The 220 V power chain

Coils are not drawn here. Both contactors are shown as power poles only; their control circuits are Fig. 2 and Fig. 3.

Fig. 2 — Control: the paddle and the main coil

The paddle's own circuit, which is pair 2 off the 110 V splitter in Fig. 4. It switches both legs, so the coil is isolated on both sides when it drops.

A locking mushroom E-stop drops into this position unchanged — push to latch, twist to release. Nothing on this sheet or any other moves; the switch is a pilot device and the only thing the chain asks of it is to break the coil circuit. Pressed, it behaves exactly like the paddle — latched off until it is twisted to release, and the mains coming back cannot close it. The two differ only when the mains fails on its own: an untouched mushroom's contacts were never opened, so the coil picks up again when the 110 V returns, where the paddle stays dropped until the green button is pressed.

Check the tab numbering on the real switch before crimping. The pairing that matters is that 13 and 14 are one pole, not 13 and 23. The tabs are stamped; it is a glance.

Fig. 3 — Control: the pump follows the spindle

The VFD's own relay is in series with the pump contactor's coil, and both sit on pair 3 so the whole pump branch is behind one breaker.

This puts mains on the VFD's control terminal strip, beside the analog. That same strip carries the 0–10 V speed reference and FOR/DCM from the interface board. Bring the coil pair in sleeved and separate, and out the opposite end of the strip from the analog pair — not bundled with it. A 0–10 V reference running alongside a switched 230 V coil is the coupling that shows up as spindle speed jitter and gets blamed on the drive.

Fig. 4 — The 110 V side: everything that survives the E-stop

Nothing on this sheet is switched by the paddle. Everything here is black and white — and under his scheme that is the tell: 110 V survives an E-stop, 220 V does not.

For wiring work, pull both pigtails. With the paddle down, more of this box is live than is dead — the whole of this sheet, the paddle's own feed, and the 220 V up to the main contactor's line terminals. Only the pairs off the 2 × 8 block are dead. Two plugs on the floor is a break you can see from inside the box.

Fig. 5 — Grounds meet at one PE bar

Everything lands on one bar and nothing daisy-chains.

A plastic box is what makes this star genuinely single-point. In a metal enclosure every mounting screw, every DIN rail foot and every supply case touching the panel is another ground path in parallel with the bar — which is the loop this arrangement exists to prevent, arriving by accident. PVC cannot make one. The cost is that nothing is bonded for free.
Not at the 240 V input, and never through a signal wire. The supplies are isolated, so the mains input is not a ground reference for the DC side. A ground that arrives through a signal conductor is a ground that carries signal current.
