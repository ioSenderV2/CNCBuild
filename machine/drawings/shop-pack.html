This pack is deliberately incomplete, and the gaps are the point. Rows shaded amber are dimensions that do not exist anywhere in the repo. They have not been invented here. Every amber row is a thing to measure or decide before the trip, and the plate it sits on cannot be drilled until it is filled in.
Standing rules for every plate in this pack
  • One datum per plate, never chain dimensions. Each drawing names a datum corner; every hole is an absolute X/Y from it. On a manual mill with a DRO the operator types absolute coordinates, and chained dimensions accumulate error.
  • Take the mating hardware, not just the plates — bearing blocks, ball nut housing, BK12, BF12, and an offcut of the 30-6060 with its corner bores. Every pattern gets checked against the real part while still standing next to the mill.
  • Extrusion corner-bore patterns are transfer-punched, not dimensioned. Those bores are extruded features whose position tolerance is looser than anything drilled in plate. A punch through the real profile beats a coordinate every time.
  • 9 mm clearance on M8 wherever a plate meets extrusion. That is the repo's deliberate figure — the joint works by friction, not bearing, and the float is wanted.
  • Close-fit holes for rail one, oversize for rail two by about a millimetre, on any plate carrying two parallel rails.

Notes

The reasoning behind the sheets. Nothing here is a dimension to work from — every number that gets drilled is on its own sheet. These explain why, and record what was tried and rejected, so neither has to sit on a page someone is working from.

SHEET 1

1.1 — The stock is 10 mm, measured. Not 3/8" (9.525) and not 1/2". The two plates nest out of one 302 mm square only because top + base + kerf closes: 100 + 200 + 2 = 302. Lay both patterns out from the factory vertical and top edges, so the bandsaw kerf never enters the pattern - it only shortens one piece's taper by about a millimetre. Laid out from the cut edge instead it becomes a ~1.5 mm offset built into a pattern that has to meet formed threads.

1.2 — ø9, not 8.2, and punched rather than measured. The bolts land in extruded corner bores, whose positions carry looser tolerance than anything drilled in plate, so a close fit is impossible in principle. The joint works by friction, not bearing - about 36 kN across eight bolts against loads of one to two kN - so the bolts never touch the hole walls. The nominal pattern is 15 and 45 in from each face; a stacked pair gives 15 / 45 / 75 / 105.

1.3 — The window is two stacked clearances, not one opening. The upper 9.525 is the interposer bar passing through; the lower 40.49 is for the casting, 27 tall. The 13.49 surplus is the room the casting is rotated into - it does not drop straight in. The rotation is fore and aft, about an axis across the window, so the clearance it consumes is HEIGHT. The width, 60.5 over a 60 mm casting, is a slip fit and nothing more: if it binds, the remedy is height.
Assembly: from behind the fin, casting upside down, stepper-bracket top into the window first, then rotate CCW until the casting lands flat on the bar.
The outboard edge is aligned on the beam face at X 20 rather than centred on the beam, which puts all the clearance inboard where the taper has already provided room, and makes the window edge flush with the 1/8" side plate's inner face.

1.4 — T2's perpendicular edge distance is 19.1, checked against the physical MDF template. The 145 mm couple with T1 is the moment restraint. Y 30 is one datum across all three plates - front fin, side plates and back plate.

1.5 — The taper is gated on spindle clearance. The spindle is confirmed to pass this plate's plane; the risk is a corner-of-envelope case - low Z, minimum Y, X near the beam. Four numbers decide it and three are unmeasured. One bandsaw pass, and it cannot be put back.

SHEET 2

2.3 — The plate's tightest edge distance. The beam's back face is flush with the plate's back edge, so the 15 is anchored and the 45 follows from the corner bores being symmetric in a 60 × 60. 15 mm of edge leaves 10.5 mm of material beside a ø9 - do not let it drift.

2.4 — Bore this first: everything else on the plate hangs off it. Its height is the Y beam's seam centreline, in the measured 45 mm gap between block rows with 5 mm each side - so there is no room for a flange or clamp ring. Y 77 is half of 154, so it is centred on the plate as well as on the block pattern.

2.5 — Where the sixteen come from. The blocks sit on the measured 45 mm gap about the bore, each 44 wide, so their centres are 44.5 either side of X 122.5. The 32 × 36 bolt pattern is the kit's own, measured. The butted pair reads 154.18 against a 154 plate - that 0.09 of overhang at each end is end cap, which carries nothing, so the pattern is centred on the plate and the Y values are round. Measure the X/Y kit, not the Z kit: they are different suppliers and have only happened to agree so far.

2.6 — Machined separately, aligned by a common WCS. The doubler is not clamped to the plate and drilled through — each part is machined on its own and the shared origin is what makes the two D rows line up. Hold the doubler the short way: X along its 50, Y along its 154, origin on the plate's bottom end and back edge, and its coordinates are then literally the same numbers 2a carries. These bolts run along X through the plate, in shear. The doubler is 154 × 50 - not 60, which overlapped the lower bearing block row by 4 mm once the block's 44 width was known.

2.7 — One root row is machined, the other is transferred. The doubler's four root holes are drilled and tapped as part of machining the doubler — Sheet 2d's R1–R4, the only edge holes that need the mill. The end plate's four are never set up at all: bolt the doubler to the plate, bolt the sole bracket up into the doubler through that first row, and the bracket's second row is then four empty holes sitting on the end plate's end face. Drill through them. A hole you transfer needs no coordinate and fits better than one you compute.
20 deep max: a ø5 doubler bolt at X 25 spans 22.5 to 27.5, so a 20 mm hole clears its wall by 2.5.

2.8 — M8 × 35, not × 40. These break out onto the face the lower Y bearing block row seats against, and a protruding bolt there would hold a block off its mounting face - the one surface on this plate that must be flat. Through 25.4 of shelf bar into 12.7 of plate, an M8 × 35 gives ~9.6 mm of engagement and stops short; an M8 × 40 protrudes ~1.6 mm. Blind-tapping used to make that impossible by construction; with a through hole the bolt length is the safeguard.

2.9 — The two plates are no longer interchangeable. Everything below the top edge is one drawing - outline, block holes, encoder bore, shelf, sole plate and doubler all common. Only the right-hand plate carries the BF12 tongue, which is a 50 mm extension above the 60 mm square that covers the upper extrusion's end, with the same 4 × M5 pattern the Y end plates use.

2.10 — 60 long, and the change deleted a hazard. The bar was 76.2 to span "the beam's 60 mm depth plus the back plate". With the beam flush, its back face is the plate's back edge, so the joining plate now sits entirely behind the plate in air. The old sheet warned that the joining plate's bottom edge had to be flush, never proud, or the gantry would sit on a plate edge in line contact. That risk existed only because the bar reached under it. Ending at Y 0 removes the failure mode by construction.

2.11 — ⚠ Check the counterbore diameter before cutting to exactly 60. V1 at Y 15 leaves 15 mm to the bar's end, which is only ~7.5 mm of wall outside a ø15 counterbore. If it reads tight on the real counterbore, 65 costs nothing and still stops short of the joining plate.

2.12 — Separated in Z, not in Y, and that is the better axis. Both sit at Y 30 because the vertical T-nut bolts hold Y 15 and Y 45 and two M8 need ~12 mm between centres - so Y 30 is the only free station. Stacking them 20 mm apart in Z resists the bar pitching nose-down, which is the load that matters.

2.13 — The tongue's X carries the interposer, and that is why it is 50. 249.5 and 267.5 put BF12 7 and 25 past the beam end — Sheet 7's own offsets before it took the interposer. The interposer is 3 off: Y1, Y2 and the X stepper end, so this screw has the same arrangement. The sign works out the same on both sheets for opposite physical reasons: the Y stepper frames hang below their beam and the interposer drops them further, while this tongue sits above its beam and the interposer lifts it — on each sheet's own frame, an addition. So 259.0 and 277.0. At the original 40 the far row then had 5.5 to the tongue's edge, which is not enough beside an M5, so the tongue went to 50 and has 15.5. Both rows derive from T_INTERPOSER, which is still nominal — caliper the 3/8" stock and they follow it.

SHEET 3

3.1 — Both rails are tapped M5, fixed. Seven holes each at a 60 pitch, 20 from each end of the 400 mm rail - and 407 − 400 leaves the 7 mm of designed overrun at the far end. Measured 10 mm of ball-free block, so 3 mm spare.

3.2 — These cannot be adjusted later. Their heads are buried under the Z rails once assembled. Tram is set here, once, at build.

3.3 — The plate exists and is dyed. Rails, BF12, the four X blocks, the Z casting and the sensor bore are all dimensioned; only the top stop is open.

3.4 — Keep the overhang short, so an impact loads the bolts in shear rather than a cantilevered lip.

3.5 — X 10 puts the support flush with the plate's X 0 end. The two holes are 46 apart, centred on Y 77 - the plate's mid - so 54 and 100.

3.6 — The cantilever is deliberate. The bottom ~150 is over the X gantry and the rest stands above it, which is how the owner's Mega V is built: 300 mm rails there give 150 over and 150 in air, and the 400 mm rails here add the height.

3.7 — ⚠ X 403 leaves 4 mm to the plate's end, so a ø4.2 drill leaves about 1.9 mm of wall. The pattern is the casting's own - spacings 13 and 30 along X, 46 across - the same six holes the interposer carries on Sheet 6, which is how both were checked.

SHEET 4

4.1 — Edge distance sets the 164. The outer M5 column sits 73.5 from plate centre and its counterbore is ~ø10, so it reaches 78.5; at 164 the half-width is 82 and they clear by 3.5. The plate also has to cover the spacers, which needs 164 on its own - two independent reasons on one number.

4.2 — Clamp bolts. They pass through the clamp, where the clearance is, and thread into this plate - ~15 N·m, 10 mm of engagement, which is 1.25 diameters and develops roughly ten times the margin the clamp reaction needs. The footprint is 122 × 55 centred on ±58, with the 100 × 25 pattern centred in it, which is what leaves 2 mm of relief at each plate end.

4.3 — Why 116 mm clamp centres and never 144. The M8 column at 50 is only 8.5 from the M5 column at 41.5, and an M5 counterbore is ~ø10 against an M8 thread at ø8 - at the same height those two features physically overlap. The clearance has to be bought vertically. At 144 the inner M8 rows would land at ±59.5 against M5 rows at −59.2 and +56.8: a direct hit at both ends, and it would have been found at the mill.

SHEET 6

6.1 — X 0 is the bar's outboard end - the end that protrudes 30 mm past the fin's outer face and goes through the window. 107.3 of the bar sits over beam, which is the only part that can reach a T-nut.

6.2 — The casting's own six holes, tapped into 3/8" = 1.9 diameters of engagement, inside the 1.5-2 band aluminium wants. The thread shears near 18 kN against an M5 class 8.8 bolt breaking near 11, so the plate thread outlasts the fastener.

6.3 — No counterbores anywhere. Row 138 leaves 12 mm to the bar's inboard end, against the ~9 mm half-head of an M8 flange bolt. Row 82 clears the casting's last tapped row at 71 by 13.6 in 2D - 11 along against the 8 lateral offset.

6.4 — X inherits these rows from Y geometry. 82 / 110 / 138 came out of the Y end - the 30 mm protrusion past the fin, the window, the casting's own tapped rows. On X the bar sits on top of the beam and overhangs its end. Check all three land over beam before drilling the third.

SHEET 7

7.1 — The length is a window, not a target. It is set by the two beams' spacing plus their end plates and is forgiving - which is why ~1205 is written with a tilde and nothing downstream depends on the exact figure.

7.2 — The same 8-bolt corner-bore pattern as every other beam joint, transfer-punched from a real extrusion offcut. 16 bolts, 8 per beam.

7.3 — Y 270 here, and it is NOT the side plates' Y 30. This plate is clamped upside down, so its Y is measured from the installed top edge: Y 270 puts the row 34.8 from the installed bottom edge, where the side plates' row is 30 from theirs. The two were one datum before this sheet was reframed and they are 4.8 apart now — deliberate, and written here so nobody 'corrects' one to match the other. The pitch differs too, because the plates do: 150 over the 1200 beam span here, 100 over 1000 on the side plates. Eight at 150 span 1050, so centring them on the beam span leaves 75 clear at each end.

7.4 — BF12 face-mounts on 4 × M5 here. Face-mounted takes 4; top-mounted takes 2. Any BF12 hole count in this repo has to name its orientation or it means nothing.

7.5 — ⚠ This gates the plate more than any hole on it. Bed size is not travel: the question is whether the two 8-bolt patterns' relative HEIGHT survives a reposition across 1200 mm. The budget is well inside 1 mm - the float the 9 mm holes give - and past it the plate wins and a beam end has to be jacked.

7.6 — Clamp this plate upside down, and the coordinates get easy. Installed, both Y beams sit near the plate's top edge and the torsion-box row near its bottom. Measured from the bottom edge every beam number would be a large subtraction off 304.8, and the two 8-bolt patterns — the ones that must agree in height across 1200 mm — would be the numbers most exposed to it. Inverted, Y1's lower-left corner lands exactly on X 0, Y 0, so its eight bores are the extrusion's own 15 / 45 / 75 / 105 pattern with nothing added, and Y2's are the same pattern mirrored off X 1200. The one cost is that this sheet's Y no longer reads the same way round as Sheet 7's own installed view - see 7.3.

7.7 — The BF12 rows carry the interposer, and the full thickness applies. The screw's near end is carried by the cast stepper frame, which sits on the 3/8" interposer and is therefore LOWER than bare metal. BF12 at the far end has to drop by the same amount or the screw is not parallel to its own beam. This sheet is clamped inverted, so lower is a larger Y: 127 and 145 become 136.5 and 154.5. Settled 2026-10-03: the full 9.525, not 4.525. The registry's NUT_FACE_BELOW_BEAM does subtract a 5 mm standoff, but only because its base figure was measured on a machine that still had one. The standoff no longer exists in the design, so there is nothing here to take back off. ⚠ T_INTERPOSER is still nominal — caliper the 3/8" stock before this plate is drilled, and these two rows follow it automatically.

7.8 — The plywood tongues are never drilled to a coordinate. The plan is to assemble the machine, set it on the torsion box, and then use the front fins, the side plates and this rear plate as drill guides to spot the plywood through their own holes. So the eight X stations here only have to clear obstructions and spread the load — nothing downstream has to agree with them, because the mating holes do not exist until the steel is bolted up and sitting in place. That is why 150 is a decision rather than a measurement, and why a hole landing a millimetre off costs nothing. It is the same logic as R5–R8 on Sheet 5: a hole you transfer needs no coordinate and fits better than one you compute.

Full reasoning, and the decisions these summarise, live in machine/*.md. If a note here and a .md file disagree, the .md file wins.
