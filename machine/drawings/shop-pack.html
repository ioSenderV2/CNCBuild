Status — what is missing and what is in progress

The pack reflects the Y end rework: cast stepper frames replaced BK12 and the M5 standoffs, the Y steppers moved to the front, all four as-built Y risers are scrapped, and the two rear risers became one 1210.73 mm steel plate. Every sheet below has been brought up to that design.

WCS ORIGIN — THE SAME ON EVERY SHEET, unless that sheet says otherwise.
It is the FRONT-LEFT-TOP corner of the part as clamped. X along the longest dimension, Y across it, Z = 0 at the top face and negative into the material. All coordinates positive in X and Y, absolute, never chained.
Each sheet states only what X 0 and Y 0 mean on that part. Three sheets override this and say so in their own header: Sheet 2d is clamped the short way on 2a's origin, Sheet 6 takes X 0 at the bar's outboard end, and Sheet 7 is clamped upside down.

This pack is deliberately incomplete, and the gaps are the point. Rows shaded amber are dimensions that do not exist anywhere in the repo. They have not been invented here. Every amber row is a thing to measure or decide before the trip, and the plate it sits on cannot be drilled until it is filled in.
Standing rules for every plate in this pack
  • One WCS origin per plate, never chain dimensions. Each drawing names its origin corner; every hole is an absolute X/Y from it. On a manual mill with a DRO the operator types absolute coordinates, and chained dimensions accumulate error.
  • Lay the extrusion bolt patterns out by scribing. Two lines, four stations ticked along each — that is the eight-hole pattern at a beam end.
  • 9 mm clearance on M8 wherever a plate meets extrusion. That is the repo's deliberate figure — the joint works by friction, not bearing, and the float is wanted.

Notes

The reasoning behind the sheets. Nothing here is a dimension to work from — every number that gets drilled is on its own sheet. These explain why, and record what was tried and rejected, so neither has to sit on a page someone is working from.

SHEET 1

1.1 — The stock is 10 mm, measured. Not 3/8" (9.525) and not 1/2". The two plates nest out of one 302 mm square only because top + base + kerf closes: 100 + 200 + 2 = 302. Lay both patterns out from the factory vertical and top edges, so the bandsaw kerf never enters the pattern - it only shortens one piece's taper by about a millimetre. Laid out from the cut edge instead it becomes a ~1.5 mm offset built into a pattern that has to meet formed threads.

1.2 — ø9, not 8.2, and punched rather than measured. The bolts land in extruded corner bores, whose positions carry looser tolerance than anything drilled in plate, so a close fit is impossible in principle. The joint works by friction, not bearing - about 36 kN across eight bolts against loads of one to two kN - so the bolts never touch the hole walls. The nominal pattern is 15 and 45 in from each face; a stacked pair gives 15 / 45 / 75 / 105.

1.3 — The window is two stacked clearances, not one opening. The upper 9.525 is the interposer bar passing through; the lower 40.49 is for the casting, 27 tall. The 13.49 surplus is the room the casting is rotated into - it does not drop straight in. The rotation is fore and aft, about an axis across the window, so the clearance it consumes is HEIGHT. The width, 60.5 over a 60 mm casting, is a slip fit and nothing more: if it binds, the remedy is height.
Assembly: from behind the fin, casting upside down, stepper-bracket top into the window first, then rotate CCW until the casting lands flat on the bar.
The outboard edge is aligned on the beam face at X 20 rather than centred on the beam, which puts all the clearance inboard where the taper has already provided room, and makes the window edge flush with the 1/8" side plate's inner face.

1.4 — These two are L-bracket holes, and the coordinates did not move when they became them. They used to pass through the fin into a front tongue on the torsion box. There is no front tongue any more — the box runs 140 past the fins instead, the fin sits on its top skin, and two L-brackets behind each fin tie it down. These are the holes their vertical legs bolt to, so the part changed and the pattern did not. T2's perpendicular edge distance is 19.1, checked against the physical MDF template, and the 145 mm couple with T1 is still the moment restraint — which is the reason to keep the pattern rather than re-pick it for a bracket. X 30 also still holds the one bolt height shared with the side plates' and the back plate's rows.

1.5 — The taper is gated on spindle clearance. The spindle is confirmed to pass this plate's plane; the risk is a corner-of-envelope case - low Z, minimum Y, X near the beam. Four numbers decide it and three are unmeasured. One bandsaw pass, and it cannot be put back.

SHEET 2

2.3 — The plate's tightest edge distance. The beam's back face is flush with the plate's back edge, so the 15 is anchored and the 45 follows from the corner bores being symmetric in a 60 × 60. 15 mm of edge leaves 10.5 mm of material beside a ø9 - do not let it drift.

2.4 — Bore it first, but it no longer sets anything. Its height is the Y beam's seam centreline, which the vertical chain puts at 122.525 - the plate's bottom end is 62.525 below the beam, and the beam is 120 - so the 122.5 drawn here is that number rounded, not an independent choice. It sits in the gap between the block rows, which is 46 by geometry. That gap is a KEEP-OUT, not something to lay holes out from: the magnetic linear encoder tape runs down it and this ø35 sensor rides over the tape, both centred on the seam. The measured 45 is kept as the conservative figure for it — under-stating a keep-out is safe — and at 45 there is 5 each side of the sensor, so no room for a flange or clamp ring. Y 77 is half of 154, so it is centred on the plate as well as on the block pattern.

2.5 — Where the sixteen come from. They used to be laid out off the measured 45 mm gap between the block rows, which put their centres 44.5 either side of X 122.5. That was the wrong driver. The blocks sit on the rails, and both rails go in the beam's outside T-slot - 15 in from each outer face of a 120 beam, so 90 apart, centres at the seam ±45 = X 77.5 and 167.5. The gap that follows is 46, not 45; the 45 was a measurement off the machine and is only conservative, never wrong, for the clearance it is used for in 2.4. The correction moved all sixteen holes 0.5, which an ø5.2 clearance on an M5 cannot absorb, so it had to be made. The 32 × 36 bolt pattern is the kit's own, measured. The butted pair reads 154.18 against a 154 plate - that 0.09 of overhang at each end is end cap, which carries nothing, so the pattern is centred on the plate and the Y values are round. Measure the X/Y kit, not the Z kit: they are different suppliers and have only happened to agree so far.

2.6 — Machined separately, aligned by a common WCS. The doubler is not clamped to the plate and drilled through — each part is machined on its own and the shared origin is what makes the two D rows line up. Hold the doubler the short way: X along its 45, Y along its 154, origin on the plate's bottom end and back edge, and its coordinates are then literally the same numbers 2a carries. These bolts run along X through the plate, in shear. The doubler is 154 × 45. Its height has an upper bound of 55.5 - the lower bearing block row's bottom edge, on this same face - and 45 sits well inside it, which is also the width of the offcut the pair comes out of. 60 overlapped that row by 4.5.

2.7 — One root row is machined, the other is transferred. The doubler's four root holes are drilled and tapped as part of machining the doubler — Sheet 2d's R1–R4, the only edge holes that need the mill. The end plate's four are never set up at all: bolt the doubler to the plate, bolt the sole bracket up into the doubler through that first row, and the bracket's second row is then four empty holes sitting on the end plate's end face. Drill through them. A hole you transfer needs no coordinate and fits better than one you compute.
20 deep max: a ø5 doubler bolt at X 25 spans 22.5 to 27.5, so a 20 mm hole clears its wall by 2.5.

2.8 — M8 × 35, not × 40. These break out onto the face the lower Y bearing block row seats against, and a protruding bolt there would hold a block off its mounting face - the one surface on this plate that must be flat. Through 25.4 of shelf bar into 12.7 of plate, an M8 × 35 gives ~9.6 mm of engagement and stops short; an M8 × 40 protrudes ~1.6 mm. Blind-tapping used to make that impossible by construction; with a through hole the bolt length is the safeguard.

2.9 — The two plates are no longer interchangeable. Everything below the top edge is one drawing - outline, block holes, encoder bore, shelf, sole plate and doubler all common. Only the right-hand plate carries the BF12 tongue, which is the stock's own far end left uncut over the upper extrusion's end, with the same 4 × M5 pattern the Y end plates use.

2.10 — 60 long, and the change deleted a hazard. The bar was 76.2 to span "the beam's 60 mm depth plus the back plate". With the beam flush, its back face is the plate's back edge, so the joining plate now sits entirely behind the plate in air. The old sheet warned that the joining plate's bottom edge had to be flush, never proud, or the gantry would sit on a plate edge in line contact. That risk existed only because the bar reached under it. Ending at Y 0 removes the failure mode by construction.

2.11 — ⚠ Check the counterbore diameter before cutting to exactly 60. V1 at Y 15 leaves 15 mm to the bar's end, which is only ~7.5 mm of wall outside a ø15 counterbore. If it reads tight on the real counterbore, 65 costs nothing and still stops short of the joining plate.

2.12 — Separated in Z, not in Y, and that is the better axis. Both sit at Y 30 because the vertical T-nut bolts hold Y 15 and Y 45 and two M8 need ~12 mm between centres - so Y 30 is the only free station. Stacking them 20 mm apart in Z resists the bar pitching nose-down, which is the load that matters.

2.13 — The tongue's X carries the interposer, because the interposer is under the casting. X 242.5 is the top of the X beam's upper extrusion, and both rows are measured up from it. The cast stepper frame at this end does not land on the beam: a 3/8" interposer sits between beam and casting, so the frame and the screw it carries both stand 9.525 higher, and the holes that mount BF12 must be drilled where the screw actually ends up. So the X is two terms, both forced — 7 and 25 above the beam top give 249.5 and 267.5 bare, plus 9.525 for the interposer, giving 259.0 and 277.0. The 7 and 25 are Sheet 7's own offsets before it took the interposer; there are three interposers — Y1, Y2 and this end — so the same screw has the same arrangement at all three.
The sign is an addition on both sheets, for opposite physical reasons: the Y stepper frames hang below their beam and the interposer drops them further, while this tongue sits above its beam and the interposer lifts it. On each sheet's own frame, measured from its own beam face, both are +9.525.
The Y columns are forced too. BF12's 46 mm column spacing centred in the tongue's 60 gives 7 and 53 — the same two columns Sheet 7 uses, and the same 7 / 53 measured on the cast stepper frame.
🔴 T_INTERPOSER is still nominal. Caliper the 3/8" stock — if it reads anything but 9.525, both rows move by the difference and so does the casting, together, which is the point: they are one assembly on one shim.
The tongue's own length is not in that chain. It has a floor and no target: it must back the whole of BF12, which is 43 tall on a 9.5 interposer, so anything over 52.5 serves and the 300 stock gives 57.5. Below that floor the block overhangs unsupported plate; above it, longer buys nothing but edge distance.

2.14 — The corner cut. Both plates lose the far-front corner on a straight line ending at X 188, Y 154 — 2a from X 242.5, Y 60, 2b from X 300, Y 60. Y 60 is where it has to start: below that is the upper extrusion's end and the plate must stay full length to cover it; above it the corner carries nothing. Clearance, computed and not measured: the nearest feature to either line is the far-upper bearing block M5 at X 183.5, Y 133.5 — 14.2 mm from 2a's line and 18.6 from 2b's, so a ~ø10 counterbore leaves about 9 mm of material on the tighter one. Nothing else comes within 20 mm: E1–E4 are all at Y 45 or below, and the ø35 bore is 79 mm clear.

SHEET 3

3.1 — Both rails are tapped M5, fixed. Seven holes each at a 60 pitch, 20 from each end of the 400 mm rail - and 407 − 400 leaves the 7 mm of designed overrun at the far end. Measured 10 mm of ball-free block, so 3 mm spare.

3.2 — These cannot be adjusted later. Their heads are buried under the Z rails once assembled. Tram is set here, once, at build.

3.3 — The plate exists and is dyed. Rails, BF12, the four X blocks, the Z casting and the sensor bore are all dimensioned; only the top stop is open.

3.4 — Keep the overhang short, so an impact loads the bolts in shear rather than a cantilevered lip.

3.5 — X 10 puts the support flush with the plate's X 0 end. The two holes are 46 apart, centred on Y 77 - the plate's mid - so 54 and 100.

3.6 — The cantilever is deliberate. The bottom ~150 is over the X gantry and the rest stands above it, which is how the owner's Mega V is built: 300 mm rails there give 150 over and 150 in air, and the 400 mm rails here add the height.

3.7 — ⚠ X 403 leaves 4 mm to the plate's end, so a ø4.2 drill leaves about 1.9 mm of wall. The pattern is the casting's own - spacings 13 and 30 along X, 46 across - the same six holes the interposer carries on Sheet 6, which is how both were checked.

SHEET 4

4.1 — Edge distance sets the 164. The outer M5 column sits 73.5 from plate centre and its counterbore is ~ø10, so it reaches 78.5; at 164 the half-width is 82 and they clear by 3.5. The plate also has to cover the spacers, which needs 164 on its own - two independent reasons on one number.

4.2 — Clamp bolts. They pass through the clamp, where the clearance is, and thread into this plate - ~15 N·m, 10 mm of engagement, which is 1.25 diameters and develops roughly ten times the margin the clamp reaction needs. The footprint is 122 × 55 centred on ±58, with the 100 × 25 pattern centred in it, which is what leaves 2 mm of relief at each plate end.

4.3 — Why 116 mm clamp centres and never 144. The M8 column at 50 is only 8.5 from the M5 column at 41.5, and an M5 counterbore is ~ø10 against an M8 thread at ø8 - at the same height those two features physically overlap. The clearance has to be bought vertically. At 144 the inner M8 rows would land at ±59.5 against M5 rows at −59.2 and +56.8: a direct hit at both ends, and it would have been found at the mill.

SHEET 6

6.1 — X 0 is the bar's outboard end - the end that protrudes 30 mm past the fin's outer face and goes through the window. 107.3 of the bar sits over beam, which is the only part that can reach a T-nut.

6.2 — The casting's own six holes, tapped into 3/8" = 1.9 diameters of engagement, inside the 1.5-2 band aluminium wants. The thread shears near 18 kN against an M5 class 8.8 bolt breaking near 11, so the plate thread outlasts the fastener.

6.3 — No counterbores anywhere. Row 138 leaves 12 mm to the bar's inboard end, against the ~9 mm half-head of an M8 flange bolt. Row 82 clears the casting's last tapped row at 71 by 13.6 in 2D - 11 along against the 8 lateral offset.

6.4 — X inherits these rows from Y geometry. 82 / 110 / 138 came out of the Y end - the 30 mm protrusion past the fin, the window, the casting's own tapped rows. On X the bar sits on top of the beam and overhangs its end. Check all three land over beam before drilling the third.

SHEET 7

7.1 — The length is a stackup, not a window, and that is what sets every X on this sheet. His stackup: the X beam is 1000, and each side adds a 12.7 end plate and a 30 rail-and-block stack, so the beams' inboard faces are 1084.38 apart. Each beam as assembled is 60 + 1/8" = 63.175 across, the side plate being outboard, so the rear plate taken flush with the side plates' outer faces is 1210.73. That is the same ~1212 that lateral-stiffness.md already named as the MAXIMUM — past it the rear plate becomes the proud member at the corner, the one direction the side tongue's face bearing cannot take — so this length is pinned to its upper bound, not pushed through it. Butting the plate between the side plates instead would give a rounder 1204.38 and put Y1's corner back on X 0, but it lands A1 and A9 exactly on their 15 mm edge-distance minimum with nothing left for a short cut; the overlap buys 3.175 of margin at the eight most loaded holes, and the beam ties both plates anyway, so the corner joint carries nothing either way. The consequence to respect: the plate's left edge is the WCS origin now, not just an edge. A9–A16 and F3/F4/F7/F8 are absolute from it, so if the stock comes back over-long, trim it to 1210.73 before clamping or add the excess to every right-hand X.

7.2 — The same 8-bolt corner-bore pattern as every other beam joint. 16 bolts, 8 per beam. Lay it out by scribing: two lines at the column positions, four stations ticked along each. A scribed line beats working off an extruded bore, whose own position error would come along with it — and these holes are ø9 on an M8 in any case, about a millimetre of float, which this joint wants because it works by friction rather than bearing.

7.3 — Y 30, the same number the side plates and the front fin carry. One bolt line across every plate that lands on the box, and from the installed bottom edge it reads as itself with no arithmetic. It took two reframings to get here: it was a round 270 measured from the installed top, which put the row 34.8 up and 4.8 off every other plate while a note called the difference deliberate — it was not — then 271.625 once the plate height was corrected, and now simply 30. The pitch no longer differs: 150 here over the 1210.73, and the side plates run 150 from 50 as well. Eight at 150 span 1050, so centring them on the plate leaves 80.365 clear at each end. The fractions cost nothing — see 7.8, these eight are a drill guide with no mating pattern to miss — so round them at the mill if it is easier.

7.4 — BF12 face-mounts on 4 × M5 here. Face-mounted takes 4; top-mounted takes 2. Any BF12 hole count in this repo has to name its orientation or it means nothing.

7.5 — ⚠ This gates the plate more than any hole on it. Bed size is not travel: the question is whether the two 8-bolt patterns' relative HEIGHT survives a reposition across 1210.73 mm. The budget is well inside 1 mm - the float the 9 mm holes give - and past it the plate wins and a beam end has to be jacked.

7.6 — One reference edge across every plate, and it is the bottom one. The bottom edge bears on the box's top skin, so it is the edge that locates the part; Sheet 9 has been dimensioned from it since the side plate's rows were corrected, and this sheet now matches. The alternative was to clamp the plate upside down, which is how it was drawn until 2026-10-07, and it was not an arbitrary choice — inverted, Y1's lower-left corner landed on Y 0 exactly, so the sixteen beam bores read the extrusion's own 15 / 45 / 75 / 105 with nothing added and nothing depending on the plate's height. 🔴 Know what the change costs, because it is real. From the bottom edge those sixteen are 196.625 / 226.625 / 256.625 / 286.625, every one of them the bought 301.625 minus an extrusion inset — so a plate that arrives off-size moves all sixteen, and these are the bores that must match extruded corner bores on two beams a metre apart. They are also the ones gated by whether the Laguna can hold one height reference across 1210.73 (see 7.5). The eight that got easier are the drill guides into plywood, which had nothing to miss in the first place (7.8). The four Y values live in the registry as RP_BEAM_ROW_1 to RP_BEAM_ROW_4 rather than as numbers on a drawing, so they re-derive if the plate height ever moves again. In X nothing changed: the bores still carry the 3.175 offset of the side plate the rear plate runs flush with (7.1), and Y2's are the same pattern mirrored off X 1210.73.

7.7 — The BF12 rows carry the interposer, and the full thickness applies. The screw's near end is carried by the cast stepper frame, which sits on the 3/8" interposer and is therefore LOWER than bare metal. BF12 at the far end has to drop by the same amount or the screw is not parallel to its own beam. This sheet is clamped inverted, so lower is a larger Y: 127 and 145 become 136.5 and 154.5. It is the full 9.525, not 4.525. The registry's NUT_FACE_BELOW_BEAM does subtract a 5 mm standoff, but only because its base figure was measured on a machine that still had one. The standoff no longer exists in the design, so there is nothing here to take back off. ⚠ T_INTERPOSER is still nominal — caliper the 3/8" stock before this plate is drilled, and these two rows follow it automatically.

7.8 — The plywood tongues are never drilled to a coordinate. The plan is to assemble the machine, set it on the torsion box, and then use the front fins, the side plates and this rear plate as drill guides to spot the plywood through their own holes. So the eight X stations here only have to clear obstructions and spread the load — nothing downstream has to agree with them, because the mating holes do not exist until the steel is bolted up and sitting in place. That is why 150 is a decision rather than a measurement, and why a hole landing a millimetre off costs nothing. It is the same logic as R5–R8 on Sheet 5: a hole you transfer needs no coordinate and fits better than one you compute.

Full reasoning, and the decisions these summarise, live in machine/*.md. If a note here and a .md file disagree, the .md file wins.

8.1 — One part made three times, and it is 4 mm not 1/4". The Y front strip came down to 1/4", breaking the old "same as its back plate" rule, and that is what made all three beams identical. 🔴 It came down again to 4 mm, because 1/4" does not fit: the bolt heads reach past the strip's edge and have to pass under the bearing block, which leaves at most 6 mm. At 4 mm the stack is 5 and clears; at 1/4" it is 7.35 and hits, both measured on a real beam. The stiffness gain quoted in gantry-beam-joint.md is the 1/4" figure and no longer applies. It is also the magnetic encoder tape surface, so its face finish matters more than its edges do.

8.2 — 45 is a fit, not a round number. It was 46, which is the GEOMETRIC gap between the bearing block rows — the 90 rail pitch less a 44 block — so the strip exactly filled the gap with nothing to spare. 45 is BLOCK_ROW_GAP, the conservative measured figure, which leaves half a millimetre each side. Past the block gap there is only 4.5 mm of height, so no width beyond this buys anything without rebating the strip thin.

8.3 — ✅ First hole at 50, then 150 pitch, on all three bolt-on plates. That is seven per row, 50 to 950, with 50 clear at each end. The pitch had been in gantry-beam-joint.md for weeks — M8 T-nuts at 150, four rows on the back and two on the front — but where along the 1000 the first one sat had never been written anywhere, which is why these rows were amber rather than wrong. Eight at 150 would have spanned 1050 and not fitted at all. Nothing was ever blocked by it: these are ø9 clearance holes into T-slots and the T-nut slides to meet the bolt.

9.1 — This plate replaced two parts. It is the full-height outboard plate and the Y back joining plate at once, grown rather than bolted alongside, which is why it carries the beam's four T-slot rows. That is also why the Y beams have no Sheet 10 part and the X beam does.

9.2 — 1/8", and an old table disagreed. outboard-plate.md's cross-section said the plate was 6.35 mm (1/4") in three places, left behind when the plate went to 1/8" steel. It does not drop into a channel — that question is dead, not open: the plate stands flat on the box's top skin and the 60 mm proud outer lamination, the tongue, laps it from outboard on a plain bolted lap. The channel had been dimensioned around 6.35 and there is nothing left to size. The delivered thickness still matters, but for the riser-proud check, not for a groove.

9.3 — ✅ The reference edge is the BOTTOM one, and that is what closed this sheet. The four T-slot rows were first drawn as 15 / 45 / 75 / 105 from the top edge, which is the extrusion's own slot grid measured down from the beam's top face. That was only right if the plate's top edge finished flush with that face — and 304.8 is a bought 12" size, not a derived one, so there was nothing guaranteeing it. Dimensioned from the bottom instead the rows become 197 / 227 / 257 / 287, the same four holes, but now a plate that comes out short loses material at the top and nothing moves. 🔴 199.8 / 229.8 / 259.8 / 289.8 is the wrong set — 2.8 too high — computed as if the plate's 304.8 finished flush with the beam top, which is the very assumption dimensioning off the bottom exists to avoid. The beam's height comes from the FIN: the window's top edge is the beam's underside at 182, and 182 + 120 lands on the fin's measured 302 exactly. The bottom edge earns the job because it is the one that bears, on the box's top skin. Sheet 10 never had this problem: at 120 tall its top edge IS the beam top by construction.

9.4 — The 9 mm hole is the slot. An earlier draft wanted the holes slotted progressively toward the ends for thermal differential. Unnecessary: ø9 on M8 is 1 mm of float per hole, several times the whole differential across a metre of plywood in a climate-controlled shop, and the Y risers were reamed to 9 for the same reason. Do not transfer that conclusion to the T-nut rows — those are a preloaded friction joint whose whole job is shear transfer, and they are a different argument.
