3 — Notches

Egg crate, continuous both ways, half-depth notches at every crossing. Every notch is 18 wide and 50 deep, and all eight ribs are the same part — same length, same four offsets.

Cut all eight at once, on the sliding miter saw

Stack the eight ribs face to face on edge — 144 across — clamp them as one block, set the depth stop to 50, and nibble each notch out in overlapping slides. Four notch positions, six or seven passes each.

  1. Register the ends against a stop block, and do not unclamp until all four notches are cut. The stack is one part for as long as it is clamped; the moment a rib shifts, that rib's notches move and nothing tells you.
  2. A sacrificial piece of the same stock on each face of the stack. It takes the blade's exit tear-out, and it gives the clamps something to crush instead of a finished rib.
  3. Plywood sub-base on the saw table, so the 50 is measured from a continuous flat surface and not across the saw's own kerf slot.
  4. Tall fence. The ribs stand 100 on edge, which is taller than the saw's own fence.
  5. Cut a test notch in an offcut first and try a real rib in it. That sets the width — nibbling means 18 is where you stop, so it is the one thing on this page that does not depend on a blade being the right thickness. Check the same test notch for a flat floor: the cut must be 50 deep all the way across, and a floor that comes out dished means the blade is dropping rather than sliding through the cut.
  6. Overlap each pass by about a millimetre and the floor needs no cleanup.

Set the depth stop against a test cut that you then measure, not off the screw.

Prove the depth on a practice pair first. Cut two offcuts the way the eight will be cut, flip one, turn it 90° and cross them at every station — pressed flat against the reference, because a released rib bows enough to lift a station off the table and show a step that is not in the part. Pressed flat, a correct pair reads even at every station: both bottom edges down, both top edges flush, notch floors just meeting.

Notch depth half, and err over rather than under. The fit across the notch is a slip fit, not a clearance: too tight splits the rib, too loose leaves no glue line.

The notches are the layout — a rib sits where the mating rib's notch puts it, not at a measured position — so of the four ways to be a millimetre out, only one touches the structure:

ErrorWhat happens
A position off by 1, on all eightNothing jams. The eight are identical, so the grid stays self-consistent and that rib line lands 1 off the drawing. Bays go 220 and 218
One rib of the eight offThat rib will not drop in — pare or recut it. This is what the stop block and the do-not-unclamp rule are for
A notch too wide by 1Slop at every crossing, so the grid stops holding itself square. Square it on the reference before the skins go on. Too narrow splits a rib
A notch too deep by 1Harmless, and the side to err on. The rib stands on the bottom skin so it cannot sink - both edges still reach both skins, and all that is lost is notch floor to notch floor, which carries nothing
🔴 A notch too shallow by 1The only structural one. It holds its mating rib up off the bottom skin, so the top skin bears on that set and sits clear of the rest of the grid
A step that closes when you press the rib flatNot an error - the rib's own bow lifting it off the reference. Check every station pressed flat, and measure depth on a released rib, never on the clamped stack

So the figure to get right at setup is the depth stop — and only in one direction. The stop block's position is forgiving, over-depth is forgiving, under-depth is not. Aim half a millimetre over and let the rib's bow live inside it.

Cut all eight from the same edge, then turn four of them over. One direction has to be notched from the top edge and the other from the bottom, and that costs nothing here because the notch is exactly half of 100: a rib turned upside down is a rib notched from the other edge. The offsets do not care either — the run is symmetric end to end, so a rib fitted backwards still fits.

So the eight stay one part right through the notching, and the only cut that tells a rib which way up it goes is the sand relief at the bottom edge, done afterwards on whichever edge ends up down.

4 — Sand ports

25.4 through both skins, 5 × 5 = 25 in each, one centred in every bay. Centres from the skin's edge, the same figures in X and Y — the box is square and the grid is the same both ways:

The grid from above, with the bottom skin's ports

The steps are not equal — 239 at each end of the row and 237 through the middle, because the end bays are wider. Walking it as a constant pitch puts the far port in the wrong place.

The bottom port takes a rubber plug fitted from below with a pull tab underneath — nothing goes in from above and nothing is attached to it. Pull it to drain, push it back to seal. The sand bearing on one plug is only 76 grams, so friction is all it needs. Fill through the top ports once, at assembly, before the bed goes down: the bed cannot come out of the assembled machine.

The tee track bed covers all but the front row of five, which lands on the open apron. 6.35 Baltic birch over the apron on double-sided tape covers those — a cover, not structure, so it comes off the day a port is used.

5 — Glue up

Six joints, and only the first two carry the sandwich.

JointWhereWhat it carries
Rib edge to skinevery rib's top edge to the top skin and bottom edge to the bottom skin, the full 1175 × 18 of each, both ends of all eightThis is the box. The skins are the flanges and the ribs carry shear between them. Starve it anywhere and the section is locally gone
Web edge to skinthe four perimeter webs, same joint, top and bottom edgesthe same, round the perimeter
Notch cheek to rib facethe sixteen crossings, face to face, 18 wide × 50 deep on each side of every notchthe two-way plate action — the reason this is an egg crate and not blocking. Face-to-face plywood, the strong orientation
Tongue to web facethe outer lamination glued flat to the inner web over the whole 202 height and full length, back and both sides, and the front cap doing the samemakes the 42 wall one piece, which is what lets the standing plates bolt through it in bearing. The biggest area in the box
Tongue to skin end grainthe tongue caps the end grain of both skins rather than standing on themcloses the edge; not a load path
Rib end to web faceeach rib's 18 × 100 end butting a perimeter web's inner face, sixteen of themWeak by nature — plywood edge onto face — and nothing relies on it. It is the joint the blocking scheme would have had at every junction, which is why blocking was rejected

Two things that look like joints and are not. Notch floor to notch floor — no glue reaches it and nothing asks it to carry anything, which is why over-depth there is free. And the post tenons, which are not glued at all: the box is lowered onto three tenons and the shoulder carries it.

Dominos — where they go, and where they must not

Where
The four web cornersTwo each, eight in all. That butt has nothing else locating it — a side web's end against the back or front web's inner face — and it is where racking shows before the skins go on. Two stop rotation as well as sliding; one does not
The outer laminationsA few per side. The tongue is a 202 × full-length face glue-up with no mechanical location at all, it floats on the glue, and it has to finish flush with the top skin. Same for the front cap. Screws from the inside do the same job
🔴 NOT in the rib endsOver-constraint, not cost. Every rib is already located twice — the notches fix it against every crossing rib, the bottom skin fixes its height — so pinning both ends into the webs is a third constraint that has to agree with the first two to a fraction of a millimetre, sixteen rib ends against sixteen crossings, with nowhere for the disagreement to go. A rib end that lands half a millimetre shy is invisible and harmless now; with a Domino in it, it is a part that will not seat. And that joint carries nothing

Cutter size and depth are a shop choice, but two things bound it: the mortise in the web's end has the whole length behind it, while the one in the mating web's face is cutting into 18 of thickness — so keep that side shallow and leave material behind it, because the tongue glues to that outer face. And the two depths together have to exceed the tenon's length.

The order, and the one step that cannot be undone

  1. Dry-assemble the grid on the flat reference — four ribs one way, four the other, into the perimeter webs. Press every crossing flat and check it seats. An egg crate self-jigs, so this is also the squareness check.
  2. Glue the grid to the bottom skin, standing on the reference. The tenon holes are already cut in that skin.
  3. 🔴 Fit the fin angles' T-nuts into the top skin from underneath, BEFORE THE TOP SKIN GOES DOWN. There is no access afterwards — ever — and the only fix then is a hole in the box. It is the one step here that cannot be undone. Nothing goes into the post tenons
  4. Glue the perimeter webs, two Dominos at each corner, then the outer laminations on their own pins, the tongues capping the skins' end grain.
  5. Glue the top skin on and clamp it hard onto the rib edges. Screws through the skin into the ribs give uniform pressure and can stay in; the 52 inch parallel clamps go across the top, one over each rib, so the pressure lands on structure and not on unsupported skin.
  6. Sand last, on site, through the top ports.

6 — Not on this list

The five legs, and they come FIRST. The base holds the three load-bearing legs in position, so it is built before the box and the legs before it. Each leg is laminated from 18 mm strips 4 ft long, trimmed square after the glue dries and cut to length off the standing base. They come off a second sheet of 18 — so the box and its legs together are one sheet of 24 and two of 18, not the two sheets the Sheets chapter counts for the box alone. Each leg finishes 72 across the machine by 86 fore and aft — the 72 is the lamination, and it runs across the machine so the base's fore-aft rails can pass through the leg as a through tenon left open at glue-up.

The two blocks beside the back centre tenon. They are location pieces inside the box, carry no load, and their thickness is free — so they come out of the scrap bin rather than off a sheet. They must be 100 tall like a rib so both skins glue to them, and no thicker than 73.5, which is the clear space each side of the tenon.
