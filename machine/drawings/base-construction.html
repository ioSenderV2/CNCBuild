Base Construction

Two grids that locate and constrain all six legs, three of which the box lands on. It is built BEFORE the box, and the legs before it — the box cannot be lowered onto tenons that are not located yet. All sizes in mm, all generated from the dimension registry.

The sheet layout is its own document — base-cutlist.html, which shows which sheet each piece comes off. The parts are here, in chapter 1, because they are read standing at the frame rather than at a sheet.

1 — Parts

62 pieces of 18 mm Baltic birch, grouped the way the base goes together — a group here is one assembly and carries everything that goes into it, so the legs bring their own fillers and foot spacers and the rails bring the caster blocks that bear on them.

Leg strips are ripped at 90 and trimmed to 86 once the lamination is dry, so everything cut from strip stock is listed at 90 wide. Every 36 member is two laminations, listed here as the 18 pieces that actually get cut — the 36 is not a choice, it is what fills a leg's slot.

The glue-up spacers are not a part. One set holds a leg's slot open while it is clamped and comes straight back out for the next leg, so it is one set off rail offcut, waxed or taped.

4 — The six legs, strip by strip

Every leg is four strips of 18 laminated face to face — 72 across — and the middle two are left out to make a 36 slot the full height of the leg. The lamination runs across the machine; the other way is the strip's own width, ripped at 90 and trimmed to 86.

🔴 Three of the blocks in that slot come back OUT. They are glue-up spacers — they hold the slot open and stop the outer strips bowing in under clamps, and at base assembly they are knocked out and a rail or a wall takes their place. They are the same 36 plywood as the two that stay, so on the bench nothing tells them apart. Cut each one from the stock that will fill that slot and keep glue off it — wax or tape.

The stack sums to the leg, and that is the check: 100 + 100 + 564 + 100 = 864, a front corner leg, which stops level with the upper grid's top. The 10 gap is air, not leg — only a leg the box lands on carries on through it, to the box underside at 874, and 116 more of tenon makes 990. The front centre leg gets there the other way: 100 + 764 + 10 + 116. If a leg does not add up, the mortise heights will not agree across the six and the frame sits twisted.

🔴 The back centre leg's slot carries the walls, not rails and a filler. The central wall fills the front 68 of it and runs 756 tall, 8 shy at the top; the back wall fills the back 18 for the full 764. So it takes one spacer, not two, and it gets no filler — the wall is already doing the filler's job, with a part that was going in anyway.

In the two rear corner legs the back 18 of the slot belongs to the back wall over its whole height, which is why their filler is 68 deep where the front legs' is 86. The spacers are the full 86: at glue-up nothing is in there yet, and a spacer that does not reach both faces is the one that lets a strip bow.

OPEN: Nothing packs the slot above the upper rail — the top 10 of a rear leg, and 126 on a leg with a tenon. It is slot by design, since the slot runs the full height and passes through the shoulder and the tenon. Whether that length wants a removable spacer at glue-up, purely to stop the outer strips bowing where the ledge will be cut, is his call.

5 — Top down on the three legs the box lands on

One cut, repeated: a band 42 wide is taken off the top of every outward-facing face, down to the shoulder. What is left standing is the tenon. Nothing is set out as a tenon — the tenon is what the band does not reach, which is why the slot can run straight through it.

🔴 The 42 is not one number, it is two jobs side by side, and that is why it is 42: the outer 24 is where the tongue stands, directly on the leg and not on the box, and the inner 18 is where the bottom skin rests. So the band is as wide as the wall that lands on it. On the plans below those are the two shades of the cut-away band, and what is left standing is the tenon.

On a rear corner leg the band off the side face swallows the whole outer strip and 24 of the slot, so the only material left inside the band is the inner strip — one tab, 18 × 44. On the front centre leg the band comes off the front face alone, both strips stand, and the slot leaves 36 between the two tabs. That island bears nothing, since it sits over the slot.

🔴 The tenon carries nothing. Every bit of vertical load is on the shoulder, and the tenon is location only. Which is why the slot passing through it costs nothing — a tenon is whichever strips stand inside the band, never the leg's full width.

A 12 spacer on each corner tenon

12 × 44 × 116, glued to the OUTER face of the tab, out of 12 Baltic birch scrap. The two rear legs only — the front centre leg's tabs butt the front wall's web with no gap at all.

🔴 Without it the box is located on a face and not on a corner. A rear tab butts the back perimeter web with nothing to spare and has 12 of air to the side web. Fill that, and the composite tenon's outer face lands on the side web's inner face, so the box's tongue corner is held both ways.

🔴 Put another way, the spacer makes the composite tenon fill the whole un-ledged band. That band is 72 − 42 = 30 across, the tab is the one 18 of it that is solid, and the other 12 is slot — which is the gap. And the band's inner edge IS the web's inner face, because the ledge is as wide as the wall and the leg's outer face is flush with the wall's. So filling the band and landing on the web are the same thing.

Where the 12 comes from the long way round, which is the answer to why it is not 18: the leg's outer face stands flush with the tongue's outer face, and the tongue is 24 where every other lamination is 18. So the leg and its slot sit 6 further outboard than an all-18 wall would put them: 18 + 36 of leg against 24 + 18 of wall leaves 12. It moves if any of those four do.

🔴 So no tenon cut in the bottom skin is a hole. Each one reaches an edge, which makes every one of them a saw cut. The two rear tenons take a corner, in from it to the tenon's two inboard faces plus the 1 a side; the front centre's pair take one notch out of the front edge, the island between them bearing nothing since it sits over the leg's own slot. The band of skin that goes with each cut is all under a perimeter web, with a 24 lamination standing outside it, so none of it shows.

How deep, and what lands on it

Cut all six legs to 72 × 86 first, then cut the shoulders. The two rear legs are the same leg mirrored, so trimmed to one size their tenon holes are identical and come off one setup, and the front centre one differs only in standing the full 72 wide.

🔴 Square faces matter more here than the width. The shoulder is a cross-cut and the wall lands directly over it, so a face out of square puts the bearing on an edge instead of on a face.

Keep the holes 1 generous a side — 20 × 46. Trimming the legs to one size makes them identical, not the assembly easy: a loaded box still has to be lowered onto three tenons.

🔴 The plate is not a shim cut to fit — all five are 24 and that is deliberate. The caster on its own stands 86.6 in a 100 band and would hang 13.4 clear of the floor, which 7 of foot could not make up. With the plate the stack is 110.6, which overshoots the band by 10.6 — so wheels down, every leg bottom is 10.6 off the floor and the assembly rolls on five wheels rather than dragging a leg.

🔴 10.6 is the LEAST the legs ever clear, and the foot only adds to it. Screwing a foot down lifts the frame, so a leg bottom rises with it — at a foot extension of e it stands 10.6 + e off the floor. Nothing ever sets a leg down, which is what standing on the feet means. So the 7 is spent lifting the wheel clear and then on the slab, and 10.6 is what a leg has to clear while rolling — a hump in the slab, or a cord under one leg.

A plate is in compression, not hanging: the structure bears down on it and the caster bears up, so its screws locate it and carry nothing. Four bear on a lower rail's underside, butted against a corner leg's face; the fifth on the central wall's bottom edge. Nothing passes under a leg's foot — the legs fill the band, which is what lets all five caster plates sit on one plane.

The machine stands on the feet. There is not enough travel to lift the wheels clear and set the legs back down, so the leg bottoms hang a few mm off the floor either way.

Level on three — the two REAR corners and the front centre. The two front corners come down to touch, not to lift: they carry nothing standing, and they are what stops the thing tipping about a line from a rear corner to the front centre.

🔴 The trio changed when the fifth caster moved behind the front centre leg. It used to be the two FRONT corners and a centre caster near the back. Level on the two front corners now and all three points sit in a line across the front, which defines no plane at all — the base would rock about it.

The fork channel is 700 wide, centred in the 1087 opening, with a guard rail each side screwed under the lower grid. That leaves 193.5 at each end of the opening, which is where the casters and their blocks sit. Each rail is a dressed 2x3 on edge, hanging 63.5 below the grid and leaving 36.5 under itself.

🔴 The plate goes hard against the leg, and what is left over is at the other end. 150 of plate off a leg face reaches 150, the guard rail's near face is at 155.4, and the air between them is 5.4. That gap is the first thing to go negative if the plate, the channel or the guard rail's thickness moves — check it before changing any of the three.

🔴 Both ends run under a lower fore-aft rail and are screwed up into it. That is the anchorage, and it is what takes a sideways hit from a fork. Each rail is 1223 — one lamination short at each end, so it covers the fore-aft rail's inner three strips, 54 of overlap, and its own ends sit 18 in from the base's outer faces. The central wall catches it again at mid-span, which halves the length it has to resist a hit over. They keep the jack centred and stop a fork ever reaching a caster, and they are guides and not bearing surfaces: as the jack lifts, the forks rise inside the channel until they bear on the grid's underside.

5b — The central wall, at both ends

The same joint twice. The wall slides into a leg's slot at each end — the back centre leg at the rear, the front centre leg at the front — and is bolted through with 3 bolts a side on the 141 quarter points of the clear band.

🔴 The wall is a plain rectangle: 1218 × 756, no notches anywhere. It used to take two at the front, to clear a cross rail lamination that ran past it. With a leg there that lamination butts the leg instead, in two spans, so nothing crosses the wall and there is nothing to cut.

No end grain shows at either end, and for the same reason both times: the piece running straight through that leg covers it — the back wall at the rear, the front cross rail's housed outer lamination at the front. The wall stops 18 shy of the front plane, which is exactly that housing's depth.

8 — Order

  1. Laminate all six legs, with a spacer in every slot. Cut each spacer from the stock that will fill that slot, wax or tape it so it does not glue in, and run it the full depth so the outer strips cannot bow inward under clamps.
  2. Trim the legs to 72 × 86 after the glue dries, and cut them to length — measuring from the mortise, not from an end, so the floor end takes the error.
  3. Cut the ledges and tenons on the three box legs.
  4. Build the two grids around the legs, then the two shear walls.
  5. Only then the box.

The mortise heights have to agree across the legs or the frame sits twisted — lay them all up on one flat surface with the spacers set off a single stop.
