Base Construction

Two grids that locate and constrain all five legs, three of which the box lands on. It is built BEFORE the box, and the legs before it — the box cannot be lowered onto tenons that are not located yet. All sizes in mm, all generated from the dimension registry.

One sheet of 18 is already cut into 20 strips of 90, which is 4 per leg. That sheet is spent and the sheet count below is for everything else. 2 of those 4 are not laminated into the leg — a leg is its two outer strips and a slot — so there are 10 spare full-length strips on top of the offcut past each leg length. Everything that fills a slot comes out of those, test material and glue-up spacers included, and a spacer cut from the stock that will fill the slot is a slot that fits by construction.

2 — The sheets

🔴 Sheet 1 is the only one with a cut list left — the leg strips and both central wall laminations are already off their sheets, and sheet 2 gives up two lower pads out of its waste. It carries the back wall and all twelve rail laminations. It is loaded from the left only, so what is left stays one whole offcut rather than a fringe of strips — worth more than a tighter nest.

The order on sheet 1 is: rip bank, wall, then the two short rails. Ten pieces at 1223 × 100 come off the left end as one bank of rips — the eight fore-aft laminations and both front cross rail outers, all the same length, all grain along the sheet. That bank is 1014.4 of the 1216 usable width, so it clears with 200 to spare, and one of the four lower pads goes in that band. Twelve rips in that bank would not: twelve 100 strips and eleven 1.6 kerfs want 1217.6, which misses by 1.6 and leaves nothing for either trim cut. So the two 1115 inner rails come off the far end instead, lying across the sheet — they are the pair housed in nothing, spanning only between the front legs.

The wall is turned. Its 1216 is the sheet's own width, so it lies across and its 764 runs along — the two cuts that trim the sheet's long edges are its two ends, and the panel takes no rip at all. That is the only orientation that fits: past the 1223 bank there is about 1212 of length left, and the wall is longer than that.

4 — The five legs, strip by strip

Every leg is four strips of 18 laminated face to face — 72 across — and the middle two are left out to make a 36 slot the full height of the leg. The lamination runs across the machine; the other way is the strip's own width, ripped at 90 and trimmed to 86.

🔴 Three of the blocks in that slot come back OUT. They are glue-up spacers — they hold the slot open and stop the outer strips bowing in under clamps, and at base assembly they are knocked out and a rail or a wall takes their place. They are the same 36 plywood as the two that stay, so on the bench nothing tells them apart. Cut each one from the stock that will fill that slot and keep glue off it — wax or tape.

The stack sums to the leg, and that is the check: 100 + 100 + 564 + 100 + 10 = 874, a rear corner leg, and 116 more of tenon makes 990 for a leg the box lands on. The back centre leg gets there the other way: 100 + 764 + 10 + 116. If a leg does not add up, the mortise heights will not agree across the five and the frame sits twisted.

🔴 The back centre leg's slot carries the walls, not rails and a filler. The central wall fills the front 68 of it and runs 758 tall, 6 shy at the top; the back wall fills the back 18 for the full 764. So it takes one spacer, not two, and it gets no filler — the wall is already doing the filler's job, with a part that was going in anyway.

In the two rear corner legs the back 18 of the slot belongs to the back wall over its whole height, which is why their filler is 68 deep where the front legs' is 86. The spacers are the full 86: at glue-up nothing is in there yet, and a spacer that does not reach both faces is the one that lets a strip bow.

OPEN: Nothing packs the slot above the upper rail — the top 10 of a rear leg, and 126 on a leg with a tenon. It is slot by design, since the slot runs the full height and passes through the shoulder and the tenon. Whether that length wants a removable spacer at glue-up, purely to stop the outer strips bowing where the ledge will be cut, is his call.

5 — Top down on the three legs the box lands on

One cut, repeated: a band 42 wide is taken off the top of every outward-facing face, down to the shoulder. What is left standing is the tenon. Nothing is set out as a tenon — the tenon is what the band does not reach, which is why the slot can run straight through it.

🔴 The 42 is not one number, it is two jobs side by side, and that is why it is 42: the outer 24 is where the tongue stands, directly on the leg and not on the box, and the inner 18 is where the bottom skin rests. So the band is as wide as the wall that lands on it. On the plans below those are the two shades of the cut-away band, and what is left standing is the tenon.

On a front leg the band off the side face swallows the whole outer strip and 24 of the slot, so the only material left inside the band is the inner strip — one tab, 18 × 44. On the back centre leg the band comes off the back face alone, both strips stand, and the slot leaves 36 between the two tabs. That island bears nothing, since it sits over the slot.

🔴 The tenon carries nothing. Every bit of vertical load is on the shoulder, and the tenon is location only. Which is why the slot passing through it costs nothing — a tenon is whichever strips stand inside the band, never the leg's full width.

A 12 spacer on each front tenon

12 × 44 × 116, glued to the OUTER face of the tab, out of 12 Baltic birch scrap. The two front legs only — the back centre leg's tabs butt the back wall's web with no gap at all.

🔴 Without it the box is located on a face and not on a corner. A front tab butts the front perimeter web with nothing to spare and has 12 of air to the side web. Fill that, and the composite tenon's outer face lands on the side web's inner face, so the box's tongue corner is held both ways.

🔴 Put another way, the spacer makes the composite tenon fill the whole un-ledged band. That band is 72 − 42 = 30 across, the tab is the one 18 of it that is solid, and the other 12 is slot — which is the gap. And the band's inner edge IS the web's inner face, because the ledge is as wide as the wall and the leg's outer face is flush with the wall's. So filling the band and landing on the web are the same thing.

Where the 12 comes from the long way round, which is the answer to why it is not 18: the leg's outer face stands flush with the tongue's outer face, and the tongue is 24 where every other lamination is 18. So the leg and its slot sit 6 further outboard than an all-18 wall would put them: 18 + 36 of leg against 24 + 18 of wall leaves 12. It moves if any of those four do.

🔴 So no tenon cut in the bottom skin is a hole. Each one reaches an edge, which makes every one of them a saw cut. The two front tenons take a corner, in from it to the tenon's two inboard faces plus the 1 a side; the back centre's pair take one notch out of the back edge, the island between them bearing nothing since it sits over the leg's own slot. The band of skin that goes with each cut is all under a perimeter web, with a 24 lamination standing outside it, so none of it shows.

How deep, and what lands on it

Cut all five legs to 72 × 86 first, then cut the shoulders. The two front legs are the same leg mirrored, so trimmed to one size their tenon holes are identical and come off one setup, and the back centre one differs only in standing the full 72 wide.

🔴 Square faces matter more here than the width. The shoulder is a cross-cut and the wall lands directly over it, so a face out of square puts the bearing on an edge instead of on a face.

Keep the holes 1 generous a side — 20 × 46. Trimming the legs to one size makes them identical, not the assembly easy: a loaded box still has to be lowered onto three tenons.

5a — The casters and the fork channel

5 leveling casters, one per leg, all of them INSIDE the 100 floor clearance rather than under the legs — which is what keeps the base's height exactly as drawn. Four sit beside a corner leg under the lower fore-aft rail. The fifth sits on the central shear wall's bottom edge, just forward of the back centre leg, which has no fore-aft rail of its own: a lamination each side of the wall's bottom band brings it out to 72 and the plate lands on that.

🔴 Every caster sits on a drop block, and the block's thickness is cut to fit. 86.6 in a 100 band leaves 13.4 between the wheel and the floor, and 7 of foot cannot make that up. The block brings the wheel just below the leg bottoms. Measure the slab under the six positions and cut the six blocks so they land within a couple of mm of each other — the 7 trims what is left.

A block is a shim in compression, not a hanger: the structure bears down on it and the caster bears up, so its screws locate it and carry nothing. Four bear on a lower rail's underside, hard against a corner leg's face; the fifth on the central wall's bottom edge. Nothing passes under a leg's foot — the legs fill the band, which is what lets all five plates sit on one plane.

The machine stands on the feet. There is not enough travel to lift the wheels clear and set the legs back down, so the leg bottoms hang a few mm off the floor either way.

Level on three — the two front corners and the centre. The two rear corners come down to touch, not to lift: they carry nothing standing, and they are what stops the thing tipping about a line from a front corner to the back centre.

The fork channel is 700 wide, centred in the 1087 opening, with a guard rail each side screwed under the lower grid. That leaves 194 at each end of the opening, which is where the casters and their blocks sit. Each rail is a dressed 2x3 on edge, hanging 63.5 below the grid and leaving 36.5 under itself. They keep the jack centred and stop a fork ever reaching a caster, and they are guides and not bearing surfaces: as the jack lifts, the forks rise inside the channel until they bear on the grid's underside.

8 — Order

  1. Laminate all five legs, with a spacer in every slot. Cut each spacer from the stock that will fill that slot, wax or tape it so it does not glue in, and run it the full depth so the outer strips cannot bow inward under clamps.
  2. Trim the legs to 72 × 86 after the glue dries, and cut them to length — measuring from the mortise, not from an end, so the floor end takes the error.
  3. Cut the ledges and tenons on the three box legs.
  4. Build the two grids around the legs, then the two shear walls.
  5. Only then the box.

The mortise heights have to agree across the legs or the frame sits twisted — lay all five up on one flat surface with the spacers set off a single stop.
