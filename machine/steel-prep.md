# Steel prep: scale off, paint on

What gets cleaned, in what order, and what goes on it afterwards. **No steel face is left bare** —
every steel part lands against 6061 and the aluminium is the anode, and the plate that faces out is
the one the wet chips land on. The scheme is **self-etch primer on the mating faces, 2K poly clear
on the show faces**, which keeps the raw steel look on everything that is visible.

## The parts

| Part | Stock | What is on it as it arrives |
|---|---|---|
| **Two Y outboard plates** | 10 gauge cold-rolled steel sheet | mill oil, and flash rust on the waterjet edges and hole walls |
| **Y rear plate**, one for both beams | 1/4" hot-rolled A36 | **mill scale**, unless it was bought P&O |
| **Two Y nut sole brackets** | 1/4" A36, the same sheet as the rear plate | **mill scale** |

Every one of them is already cut — the steel went to the waterjet and none of it is on the mill
trip — so prep and paint happen before any of it is bolted up.

SETTLED: **It is done at home, in a can, not sent out for powder.** Powder would come back finished
and opaque, and the show faces are staying raw steel.

## 1 — Degrease before the first abrasive touches it

FLAG: **Oil first, abrasive second, and not the other way round.** Cold-rolled sheet ships with a
rust-preventive oil on it. Sand it first and the oil is driven into the surface, nothing bonds to it
afterwards, and there is nothing to see — the plate looks the same as one that was done properly.

Acetone or denatured alcohol on a rag, changing rags as they load up. Or hot water and dish soap
with a scrub pad, then towel dry and a heat gun pass straight away — bare wet steel starts rusting
in minutes.

## 2 — Take the surface down

| Part | Tool |
|---|---|
| **1/4" hot-rolled** — rear plate, sole brackets | **Jelly to loosen it, then abrasive at 60–80.** 80 on the orbital dry is the controllable version; 60 in a geared mode or a 60–80 flap disc is the fast one, and the grinder will dish the plate if you lean on it |
| **10 gauge cold-rolled** — outboard plates | 120–180 grit, or a maroon abrasive pad. There is no scale here; you are only putting tooth on it for the primer |
| **Every waterjet edge and hole wall** | abrasive pad or a nylon brush, and get inside the holes |

The waterjet cuts wet, so the edges and hole walls carry light flash rust even when the faces look
clean.

**Mill scale does not dissolve, but it can be loosened first.** Phosphoric acid — naval jelly, prep
gel — barely touches the scale itself, which is mostly magnetite and is pickled industrially with
hydrochloric or sulfuric for that reason. What it does instead is get through the cracks in the
scale and attack **the steel underneath**, undercutting it. The scale loses its grip and the bulk
then comes away under a maroon pad that could not have touched it dry.

So on a heavily scaled face the order is **jelly first, pad to take the bulk, abrasive for what is
left** — the acid is a softener for the abrasive, never a substitute for it.

FLAG: **What the acid and the pad leave behind is the hard part, and neither will shift it.** The
remaining islands are scale the acid did not get under, and a maroon pad cannot win against them:
the scale is harder than the steel around it, and a non-woven pad conforms, so it polishes the soft
surround and rides over the patch. **That is the 80 % bare face with dark grey islands in it**, and
more of either tool does not change it.

**Go coarse enough to cut.** 120 and 150 burnish scale and load up, which reads as the scale being
invincible. 80 cuts it; 60 in a geared mode cuts it fast.

**On a dual-mode sander, come back up out of the aggressive mode.** Geared forced rotation leaves
deep swirls that are glaring under clear. 60 to break the scale, then 80, 120, 180 — the whole face
at each step, and the finishing grits in plain random-orbit.

FLAG: **Use a ceramic or zirconia disc on steel, not wood abrasive.** Ordinary aluminium oxide
glazes almost at once at 60, which reads as the scale winning again.

FLAG: 🔴 **Empty the dust extractor before sanding steel, or fit a fresh bag.** Coarse grit in a
geared mode throws hot particles, and hot steel into a bag holding fine wood dust is a vacuum fire.
Steel dust is conductive and abrasive as well, so it is hard on the extractor either way.

FLAG: **Take the whole face to one grit, not just the patches.** Spot-treating is what produces the
patchwork. A face worked three different ways reads as three different surfaces under clear, and the
sheen still changes under primer.

**And it all has to come off whichever face it is.** Self-etch primer is specified for bare metal —
it works by etching the substrate and cannot etch scale, so primer over a scale patch is sitting on
something that is itself only mechanically attached. Under clear it is worse: moisture gets beneath
scale eventually and lifts it.

### The stains a maroon pad will not take out

A pad or a sander leaves the faces clean except for the odd mark that looks like a water stain and
stays. **Four different things look identical, so test before working on them:**

| Test | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Is the mark dark grey, hard, and on a hot-rolled part?** | **Mill scale**, and it is the one case the rest of this table does not fit — it ends at abrasive, whatever loosened it first. See above |
| **Acetone on a white rag** — if the rag colours, or the mark's edge moves | It is **oil residue**, not oxide. Denatured alcohol is weak on rolling oil, and a maroon pad burnishes a ring rather than lifting it. This is the one that kills paint adhesion |
| **150–180 grit on a hard block**, a dozen strokes — if it clears | It was only on the surface. A maroon pad is non-woven, so it conforms, polishes the high spots and rides over anything sitting below them. A rigid backing cuts flat and reaches it |
| **It survives both** | It is **in a shallow pit**. Water sat there and the oxide went in below the surface |

FLAG: **Stop sanding a pitted stain.** Reaching the bottom of the pit means taking all the metal
around it down to that depth, which is real thickness off the panel for a mark that paint covers.

**Those go to phosphoric acid instead** — naval jelly for spot work because the gel stays where it
is put, Ospho or a prep-and-etch for a wipe-on, or Evapo-Rust and citric acid, which chelate rather
than etch and so leave the good metal alone. For a stain in the middle of a face that is already
good, soak a paper towel, lay it on, and cover it with plastic so it cannot dry out.

The acid turns the oxide into iron phosphate — a grey-black inert layer, and **a better primer
substrate than the bright steel beside it.** So treating a stain improves the panel rather than
repairing it, and the patch staying darker than its surround does not matter under primer.

FLAG: **Naval jelly comes off at 5 to 10 minutes, and 15 is the label maximum.** Longer does not
dissolve more — it just leaves a heavier, darker phosphate layer, which is invisible under primer
and a visible patch under clear. If it has hardened on the surface, **fresh jelly on top of the hard
stuff softens it**; do not dry-scrape it off a face you intend to show. Rinse with plenty of fresh
water, and repeat the whole cycle rather than leaving one application on longer.

FLAG: **Rinsed with water, it flash rusts in minutes** — faster than before, because the surface has
just been stripped chemically clean. Towel, heat gun until it is dry through and not just dry on
top, and coat it the same session. **Do not rinse a face you are not ready to coat.**

FLAG: **Gloves on anything bare, and that includes carrying a panel to the bench.** A hand mark
etches into clean steel and comes back as a cluster of finger-sized blotches that the pad will not
lift. Once a face is handled bare it keeps making new ones.

### When a face will not come uniform, go uniform instead of brighter

A metre-long panel hand-sanded does not come up evenly bright, and chasing that is what eats the
evenings. **What makes raw steel look deliberate under clear is a consistent surface, not a clean
one** — the marks stop reading as marks when there is no polished field for them to contrast
against.

| | |
|---|---|
| **One grit, directional, whole face** | Finish everything at 180 with the strokes running the same way along the panel's length |
| **Blasting** | Glass bead or fine aluminium oxide gives an even matte grey and pulls oxide out of pits that no abrasive reaches. The textbook substrate for coating, and it ends the question — at the cost of equipment, mess, and steel that flash rusts faster than anything else here, so coat it at once |

**On a mating face none of this matters.** It only matters where the clear is going.

## 3 — Deburr while it is still bare

The waterjet leaves a lip on the exit side of a hole. Hand countersink or a deburring tool, both
faces of every hole, and file the sheared edges.

FLAG: **Do it now.** A hole opened out or an edge filed after the paint is on is bare steel in the
one place that is hardest to reach again.

## 4 — Solvent wipe, then gloves

Acetone or lacquer thinner on a lint-free rag, immediately before the primer goes on. **Nitrile
gloves from that wipe onwards.** A fingerprint is salt and oil, and it is reliably where the first
bloom comes through the film.

## 5 — Prime inside the hour

Freshly abraded steel flash rusts in hours. The window is short enough that the can wants to be
shaken and ready before step 4, not after.

**Rust-Oleum Automotive Self Etching Primer, dark grey.** A rust converting primer goes on first
anywhere pitting is left after step 2.

| | |
|---|---|
| Coats | **2 or 3 thin**, 2 minutes apart |
| Dry to touch | 10 min |
| Dry to handle | 15–30 min |
| Before the topcoat | **30 min minimum** |
| Before dry sanding | 3–4 hours |
| Conditions | 50–90 °F, humidity under 85 % |
| Coverage | **10–12 sq ft a can** — the mating faces and edges alone are about a canful, so have three |

FLAG: **It is a tie coat, not a finish.** Self-etch is thin and porous by design — it bites bare
metal and gives the next layer something to hold. Dark grey looks finished enough to stop at, and
on its own it is not the moisture barrier that is the whole job at these joints. Something goes
over it, even cheap enamel on a face nobody sees.

FLAG: **Nothing gets sprayed in a room with the mining rigs running.** They move enough air to lay
dust into wet primer through the ten minutes it takes to flash, and an aerosol puts a solvent vapour
cloud around energised electronics with fans in it. Spray elsewhere, or shut them down and
ventilate first.

FLAG: **A sacrificial zinc-rich primer is the wrong choice at these joints.** What is wanted between
steel and aluminium is an insulating film, not a more active metal put deliberately into the
circuit.

## 6 — Two finishes, and no bare metal left anywhere

The show faces stay raw steel under **2K poly clear**. The mating faces get the primer. Nothing is
left bare: the outboard plate is the chip barrier, so it is the part that actually gets wet.

| Face | What goes on it |
|---|---|
| **Mating face** — against the beam | Self-etch primer, then anything over it. Nobody sees it, so cheap enamel does |
| **Show face** | 2K poly clear, straight onto the bare steel |
| **Edges and hole walls** | **Clear, not primer.** They take the thinnest film and are where failure starts |

**The edges go to the clear and the primer stops at the mating face.** Wrapping grey primer round
onto a show face leaves a band you can see through the clear, and the clear seals the edge just as
well. Two thin coats beat one heavy one either way — a heavy coat pulls away from an edge as it
flows.

FLAG: **Nothing goes under the clear that you are not keeping.** Every stain, fingerprint and bit of
flash rust left on a show face is sealed in and permanent, and there is no second attempt. Settle
step 2 on those faces before the can is opened. This is the one place the stains stop being
cosmetic.

FLAG: **There is no primer under the clear, so the clear is the only thing holding.** Bare steel is
the weakest substrate a 2K urethane gets asked to grip — the sanding key is all it has, and a breach
lets rust creep under the film where it cannot be reached. Which is the price of the raw look, and
it is paid in keeping the surface clean before spraying rather than in anything added afterwards.

**Order it so the clear is one session.** Prime every mating face first and leave them to cure. A 2K
can is activated once, has a pot life measured in a day or two, and cannot be saved — so every show
face gets done in one go, after the primer is hard.

FLAG: **2K hardener is isocyanate.** It is a respiratory sensitiser, the kind of exposure that makes
the next one worse rather than the kind you get used to. Cartridge respirator at the absolute
minimum, strong ventilation, and nowhere near the mining rigs.

## Bare-metal protectants are not primer

A graphene or wax film — Carbon Coat, Boeshield T-9, paste wax — protects a surface that has to
**stay bare**: a cast iron table, a bed, a tool. It is a thin film, it is meant to be renewed, and
standing water still gets through it. None of that is what a steel plate bolted flat to aluminium
for a metre needs, and the film has to come off again before primer would stick to it.

**Use one only on steel that is waiting**, and plan on removing it:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Carbon Coat** | the best of the three against ambient humidity on bare metal, and it is a friction reducer, so nothing primes over it |
| **Boeshield T-9 or LPS-3** | thin waxy film, months indoors |
| **Paste wax** | dry to the touch and the easiest to take off again, with mineral spirits |
| **Lanolin** | longest hold, messiest |

## Where the coating stops

Steel meets aluminium in three places on this machine and only one of them gets coated. **The cell
needs a liquid path bridging the two metals** — without one, the metals being far apart does
nothing. So the joints rank by wet and by crevice, not by the pairing:

| Joint | What it gets |
|---|---|
| **Outboard plate to beam** | **Coated.** A metre of face contact forming a crevice, on the part the wet chips land on, with a seam that traps swarf its whole length |
| **Casting foot to interposer bar** | **Nothing at the face.** It hangs under the beam at the Y end — sheltered, small, and the foot is a bearing face |
| **HG blocks to gantry plates** | **Nothing, ever.** Dry, enclosed, and oiled by the rail they ride |

FLAG: 🔴 **Never put a coating between a bearing block and its plate, or under the casting's foot.**
Those mounting faces are ground reference surfaces and the machine's geometry is set off them. A
film creeps under bolt load, is never the same thickness twice, and tilts the block — trading a
corrosion risk that is not there for a geometry error that cannot be found afterwards. The casting's
foot is worse again: it is stepped, and whether the bar holds it off its bearing face is still open.

**Where something is wanted at one of those joints, seal the perimeter after assembly.** Torque it
metal to metal, then run paint or sealant round the outside of the seam. That stops water reaching
the crevice, which is the actual mechanism, and it touches nothing that locates anything.

**Coat the cast iron everywhere except the faces that seat and the motor register.** Not for the
dissimilar metals — bare cast iron rusts on its own, and rust takes more room than the metal it grew
from, so a rusting edge can jack a joint open from outside. Mask the foot, spray the rest.

**The control on all of it is that this stays a dry-or-mist machine.** Nothing above changes unless
that does, and then the mating faces get isolated as well as coated.

## While they wait

Store the plates **on edge, off the concrete.** A slab wicks moisture into anything laid flat on it,
and the down face goes first.

## One that belongs at assembly

FLAG: **Paint creeps under sustained bolt load, so a painted joint loses bolt tension.** Re-check
the torque on the outboard plates and the rear plate a few weeks after they go on.
