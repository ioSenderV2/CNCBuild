# The torsion box, as built

**Measured off the parts, not taken off the drawing.** Where this disagrees with the registry, this
file is what the remaining parts get cut to — the registry's figures are what the box was drawn at.

## The back outer lamination is 1211, and the corner is reversed

| | As built | Drawn |
|---|---|---|
| Back outer lamination | **1211**, one piece, glued **flush at both ends** | {{BACK_LAM_L}}, two pieces butted |
| Side outer laminations, as cut | **1211** each | {{SIDE_LAM_L}} |
| Back corners | **a 24 × 24 × {{TONGUE_FULL_H}} hardwood filler in each**, two off | the back lamination lapping both sides' end grain |

A 1211 back lamination spans the top skin exactly and, flush at both ends, sits **between** the two
side laminations' inner faces rather than over their ends. So the back corner is a butt, and the
sides' end grain stands at the two back corners instead of being capped.

**The lap is bought back with a hardwood filler in each corner** — his call, machined to fit. Each
void is a **24 square column the full height of the lamination**: 24 across by 24 fore-and-aft, the
24 being the tongue's own thickness in both directions, and the full height because the lamination
caps both skins' end grain and stands {{BOX_TONGUE_PROUD}} proud of the top skin.

**Grain direction matters more than the fit.** Run the filler's grain **vertically**, the way the
plywood laminations' face grain runs along the wall: a filler with its grain across the corner is
end grain to the glue line on both faces, which is the weak way round in the one place the corner
has no lap.

**The back tongue's bolt line is unaffected.** Eight bolts at X 75 on a 150 pitch run 75 to 1125,
well inside the 1211.

## The two back pieces become the second side

The back's {{BACK_LAM_PIECE_L}} and {{BACK_LAM_PIECE_SHORT}} pieces are reassigned: one side
lamination is the remaining 1211 piece, and **the other side is made from the two back pieces butted
end to end** and cut to length. The front cap keeps its own pair.

**The butt is sound and it is where it already wanted to be:** the inner web behind it is one
continuous piece, so the joint is staggered against solid material, which is what laminating a wall
is for.

## The order the walls actually went on

1. **Back**, the 1211 piece, flush at both ends.
2. **Left side**, from the two back pieces butted, with a **1/4 gap at the back** set off a
   waterjet sole plate used as the spacer against a backer board. Rearmost piece first, second piece
   cut to fit flush with the front.
3. **Right side**, the remaining 1211 piece, same 1/4 gap at the back. **It therefore stands about 6
   proud at the box's front edge and gets trimmed off that end before glue-up** — the front cap beds
   on that face.
4. **Front cap**, the 630 and 629 pieces, 142 tall against the back and sides' 202.

**Clamping is screws from inside the cavity**, not clamps across the box — a 52" parallel clamp
cannot reach the middle of a side with the box on edge. Jug weight held the back lamination down
while the screws went in and **a 4 ft level lays flat on it**, so that glue line closed.

### Setting it up and reading it

1. **One plate bolted hard** - to its blocks and to the beam end both.
2. **The other bolted hard to its blocks only**, its M8 backed off so the plate is not clamped. **The
   gap between that beam end and that plate's face is the reading.**
3. **Start at the FRONT**, where the span is tightest, with the gap nearly shut. Travelling rearward
   opens it, which is the direction the room is in.
4. **Push the pair the full travel.** The change in the gap IS the non-parallelism - sign, amount and
   station, one to one, no amplification.

🔴 **THE SHELF BAR IS WHAT MAKES THE LOOSE END HONEST.** Without it the beam hangs on the backed-off
bolts and cantilevers off the tight plate. Sitting on a bar at each end it is simply supported and
slides freely along its own axis, which is the one freedom the measurement needs and the only one it
needs.

SETTLED: **The bars are the real parts, but they sit where the JIG's beam sits, not at
{{SHELF_BAR_TOP}}.** The bar's own job on the real plate is to carry the X beam's bottom extrusion at

## If the toe is real, the side tongue is the only thing that can move

**The lateral chain across the machine is face bearing at every step**, which is why no amount of
bolt loosening helps:

| | |
|---|---|
| Tongue inner face | **bears on** the outboard plate's outer face |
| Plate inner face | **bears on** the Y beam's outboard face |

{{BOX_W}} was rounded DOWN from the {{MACHINE_OUTER_W}} envelope *for* this - so the tongue beds
hard on the plate where the bolts are rather than opening a gap in the lap. A ø9 hole on M8 floats
only in a direction where no face is already in contact, and across the machine there is one at
every joint. **The tongue is the stop, and taking material off its inner face is the only way a beam
front goes outboard.**

### How the material comes off

🔴 **It is a beams-off, plates-off job**, and that is the gate on the whole thing rather than the
cutting. The tongue stands {{BOX_TONGUE_PROUD}} proud; the outboard plate against it is 301.625 tall
and the beam is above that, so any tool riding on the tongue's top face has its body swinging
through where the plate and beam now are.

**A router with a long straight bit.** A saw is out on the offcut alone: a 1 mm sliver
{{BOX_TONGUE_PROUD}} tall and {{SIDE_TONGUE_L}} long will flex into the blade and pinch, and that
sliver is what would set the finished face. A router takes the same millimetre off as chips. **The
bit in hand has 2.5 inches of cutting length - 63.5 against the tongue's {{BOX_TONGUE_PROUD}}
standing height**, so it takes the full height in one pass with 3.5 to spare.

**The straightedge is a 3 inch strip of 18 mm plywood, screwed down to the tongue's TOP face** - his,
2026-10-25, and it replaces the track-saw rail this section used to call for. It does three jobs at
once: its inboard edge is the pattern the bit's bearing follows, its 3 inches carry the router base
over a tongue only {{T_BOX_TONGUE}} wide, and screwed into the tongue it cannot creep mid-cut. The
bit sits at the strip's edge, so about half the base is on the strip and half over the
{{BOX_TONGUE_PROUD}} drop to the skin - the 3 inches is the half that has to be there.

🔴 **18 AND NOT 1/2 INCH, AND THE 3.5 OF SPARE CUTTER IS WHY.** With the depth set so the cutter just
kisses the top skin and takes none of it, the cutter's top - and therefore the bearing's bottom - sits
**14.5 below the strip's top face**. An 18 strip has 14.5 of thickness above that and the bearing runs
entirely on plywood. A 12.7 strip is **1.8 short**: the bearing would hang below the strip and ride
the tongue's OLD face, which is the face being removed, and the cut would come out parallel to the
error instead of correcting it. The cutter trims the strip's bottom 3.5 as it passes and that costs
nothing - the band the bearing runs on is above it.

NOTE: **Err shallow on the depth.** The plate stands on the top skin; a cut into it cannot be undone,
and the corner where the new face meets the skin is a chisel job anyway.

SETTLED: **THE REAR PLATE STAYS BOLTED ON** - his, 2026-10-25, which stops the router short of the
tongue's rear end by roughly a router base. **It is the cheapest band on the tongue to lose:** the cut
is 1.37 over {{SIDE_TONGUE_L}}, so it asks **0.137 per 100 mm** forward of the rear end - about 0.12
over a 90 band and zero at the end itself. 🔴 **WHAT IT LEAVES IS A STEP, NOT A TAPER**, because the
pass simply stops, and a flat plate bears on a step where it would ride a taper. Feather the band out
by hand; a tenth needs no precision, only no edge. **And step 7's jig reading is the check** - a band
holding the plate's rear end off shows up there as a reading above 1.13.

🔴 **THE STRIP'S INBOARD EDGE IS THE FINISHED FACE**, because a top-bearing pattern bit cuts flush
to it. That retires the two things that could have put this cut on the wrong end: there is no router
offset to find and no shim whose direction can be read backwards. Mark the line on the tongue's top
face off the tongue's own inner arris - **nothing at the rear end, 1.37 in at the front end** - screw
the strip to it, and the taper is the cut.

**Whatever is added to BOTH marks is width, not toe.** The difference between the two ends is the
whole of the toe correction; a constant on top of it only opens the span, which is the direction the
absolute wants and comes off what the X end plates were going to give up. The strip's edge wants to
be a sawn or jointed edge, because it is now the only reference in the job.

**Chisel the inside corner last.** The plate stands ON the top skin and the tongue laps it, so
anything left in the corner where the new face meets the skin holds the plate's bottom edge out and
it rocks. A straight bit leaves a fillet there whatever you do. Chisel it, check with a square, and
the plate then beds over the full {{BOX_TONGUE_PROUD}}.

NOTE: **A bit cuts full depth the whole run, and the one end it cannot reach is the end that wants
nothing.** There is no entry ramp to work around - the bit goes to depth and stays there. At the
front it runs off into {{BOX_FRONT_OVERHANG}} of clear skin. At the rear the side tongue runs into
the back tongue, so the last half a bit diameter in that corner is a chisel job - and the rear is
the **wide** end, where the taper is already at nothing.

NOTE: **What it costs, in one line.** A millimetre off the inner face takes the lap's bolt bearing
from {{T_BOX_TONGUE}} to 23 and moves the ø9 hole's edge a millimetre nearer the face. Neither
matters at that size, and the 145 mm couple into the box is untouched because it is the lap that
carries it.

FLAG: **Two things that are NOT the recovery**, both ruled out on the geometry:

- **A tapered shim under a Y rail.** A shim can only add thickness, and thickness under a Y rail
  pushes that rail inboard. The front is the narrow end, so the taper would have to come out by
  shimming the REAR - closing the gap everywhere, when the absolute is already short. It fixes the
  taper by making the harder problem worse.
- **Cutting the tongues off and replacing them.** Nothing replaces them: they are the lateral load
  path into the box and the only thing locating the plates across the machine. The cut is a skim of
  the face, not a removal of the part.

## 🔴 THE TAPE READS ABOUT 2 SHORT ON AN INSIDE MEASUREMENT - and that was the whole discrepancy

**The same span, two instruments.** Side plate inner face to inner face, taken at the FRONT, 60 mm
above the skin, against the plates' own edges rather than as a hooked inside measurement:

| Instrument | Reading |
|---|---|
| Tape | **1201** |
| 50 inch steel ruler | **1203** |
| The chain, on a 1210 skin | **1203.1** |

**The ruler and the arithmetic agree to a tenth, and the tape is 2 light.** Every tape reading taken
during this is carrying that offset. Corrected: beam inner faces about **1083**, block faces about
**1023** - not the 1081 and 1020.5 that were taped.

**So the real shortfall against the X assembly's 1024.38 is about 1.4, not 3.4 - and 1 mm of it is
the 1210 skin against the drawn 1211.** It comes off the X end plates, 0.7 a face, on plates that are
uncut and have to be faced anyway.

🔴 **DO NOT BUY A LASER MEASURE FOR THIS.** A laser distance meter runs about plus or minus 1.5 to 2,
which is worse than the ruler and nowhere near what this needs. **The instrument order here, worst to
best: tape, ruler, then the X beam itself between the block faces with feeler gauges reading the
leftover** - which contains no absolute measurement at all and is the one to use for the end plates.
**Above all of them: the carriage jig's gap read with gauge blocks by fit**, which has no absolute
and no scale read either.

NOTE: **This is the general lesson, not a one-off.** Every figure that came off a caliper, a gauge
panel, a triangle or a ruler held up. The only ones that moved were the tape's.

🔴 **Which leaves exactly one unverified link in the chain, and it is the tape.** Everything else is
now measured with a better instrument: the stacks with calipers, the box with a gauge panel, the
plates with a precision triangle, the bore stations by sixteen bolts spinning in. **The only figure
resting on a tape hooked across a metre is the block gap itself.**

OPEN: **So take the one measurement nobody has taken: the gap between the two Y beams' INNER FACES.**
A shorter reach, onto flat extrusion faces, and it decides the whole question.

- **Reads 1084** - the chain is right, the block-gap readings were the error, there is no shortfall,
  and nothing needs machining.
- **Reads 1081** - the beams really do sit 3.38 closer than {{Y_BEAM_GAP}}, which is more than the
  9 mm clearance on the rear bores can account for, so the back plate's bore stations are not where
  they are drawn.

NOTE: **Nothing on the X gantry is cut**, so a correction is still cheap either way - but the X beam
is not free: it is {{BEAM_LEN}} and the three front joining strips arrived at exactly that to match it.

## The tongue line is M8 LOCK NUTS, not acorns

**The acorn nuts bottomed out on their thread before seating**, with M8 fender washers under both
head and nut. He went to **M8 lock nuts**, which is what the outboard plate's own table in
[`../machine/assembly.md`](../machine/assembly.md) already called for: from outboard, washer, tongue,
plate, lock nut on the inboard face.

**That retires the acorn arithmetic on this line.** {{ACORN_NUT_DEPTH}} is a hard ceiling - past it
the bolt lands on the dome and the joint never clamps however hard the nut is turned, which is the
failure you cannot see from outside. A lock nut has no dome, so the ceiling and everything derived
from it stops applying.

SETTLED: **The rear plate's THIRD fender washer comes off.** It existed only to lift an acorn far
enough that an M8 x 45 would not bottom. With a lock nut there is nothing to bottom on, and the bolt
length comes back as protrusion past the nut, which is what a nylon collar wants.

**Two fender washers stay**, head and nut. That is the bearing-area reason, not a locking one - this
is the line whose plywood creeps under the bolt, and the standing answer to that is re-torque.

## Drilling the tongue holes: go in from outside

**A 9 mm bit cannot be brought in square from inside** - the hole sits 30 mm above the skin and no
drill that holds a 9 mm bit gets that low, so it enters at a downward angle and shaves the entry
edge. It does not wander, and the angle costs nothing: this line is a CLAMP and not a locator, so
the hole is pure clearance and nothing bears on its wall. The locating is the T-slot line against
the beam and the sixteen bores at the beam ends.

**The tongue is the outboard layer, so its outside face is in free air.** Transfer-punch through the
plate's hole, then drill the 9 mm from outside, square, with the whole drill clear. Or pilot small
from inside - a stubby 3 mm in a hex holder fits where a 9 mm will not - and open out from outside.

## Both casting windows are open-sided - one by accident, one on purpose

**The casting went in at the RIGHT rear corner.** That window has no outboard side: it fell at the
plate's own end, where the bought plate is {{REAR_PLATE_SHORTFALL}} short of the flush envelope, so
the strip outboard of it was never there. With the side plate coming in alongside there is still
enough slop for the casting to pass.

SETTLED: **The SENSOR goes on after the casting is in, not before - and the order is forced.** The
casting has to go through with the interposer bar as one unit, because screwing the casting to the
bar in place means driving its six M5 upside down from inside, which is not a job anybody wants. The
bar-and-casting unit passes; the unit **with the sensor already mounted does not**. So the sensor
mounts separately once the casting is in, which is what it was designed for.

**The LEFT rear window is enclosed and the casting will not pass it** - only just, but it will not.
SETTLED: **The vertical bar outboard of that window gets cut out**, making both ends alike.

**It costs nothing, for three reasons:**

- **The window was never a locating feature.** The casting is held by its six M5 into the interposer
  bar; the opening is clearance. The original fin window's note already said the width was a slip fit
  and nothing more, and that if it binds the remedy is height.
- **The right-hand end is already open** and was accepted, so this is symmetry rather than a new
  compromise.
- **Nothing loaded is in that strip.** Y1's beam bores are inboard of it at columns 15 and 45, the
  torsion-box bolts run along the bottom edge, and BF12 taps into the inside face.

🔴 **Keep the plate's LEFT EDGE continuous above and below the window.** Sheet 7's X 0 is that edge,
set on the Y1 extrusion's outboard face, and every hole on the plate is absolute from it. Cut the
window's own height only - take the full height of the plate and the thing the whole drawing is
dimensioned from is gone.

SETTLED: **Done, and BOTH sides of the left window had to come back.** The outboard bar was cut out
with a 3 inch metal cut-off wheel, and the **inboard** edge then had to be ground back as well,
because with the bar gone the **side plate** does the blocking the bar used to. The casting is in.
**It is a very tight fit and that is accepted.**

🔴 **The window's WIDTH now has no margin left.** It is an as-built limit rather than a drawn
clearance: there is little steel outboard of it and the side plate is hard against the other side. So
anything that later changes the casting's width or its position across the machine needs the window
opened again, and there is not much left to open. **The window's HEIGHT is unaffected** - it is set
on the beam underside, which is why the interposer bar going to 1/2in would not move it.

NOTE: **Paint the ground edges.** Bare steel in a corner that gets wet, next to aluminium.

## The tee track bed's end fixing, as it will actually be built

SETTLED: **FOUR screws a side, not eight.** Those screws only stop the bed walking - it is bedded
flat on the box and gravity holds the stack down - so the count was never structural. **The thing
that settles it is the access hole**, below.

SETTLED: **M5 x 20 SOCKET HEAD.** His, off the parts: button head and flange head both foul the top
skin. The hole centre is {{TT_BED_END_HOLE_Z}} above the skin and the head sits over the plate's
bottom edge, so anything wider than the {{TT_BED_SPACER_OD}} spacer starts to interfere. Length is
unchanged - 20 reaches 11.57 into a hole tapped 15.

🔴 **EVERY SCREW NEEDS A 14 mm ACCESS HOLE THROUGH THE 24 mm TONGUE.** The tongue is the outermost
layer, so the screw goes tongue, plate, spacer, panel end - and nothing can be driven without a bore
to reach through. This had not been noticed anywhere. Eight access holes is acceptable; sixteen is
not, which is why the count came down.

**The order is forced:** the hole stations are not known until the bed is in, and the bores have to
be made before the side plate goes on with its beam attached.

SETTLED: **Measure once, lay out twice, and skip the transfer.** With the bed in and the side plates
OFF, each panel's tapped holes are visible from outside. Measure their stations along the length -
that is the only unknown - then lay out BOTH the tongue's 14 mm access and the plate's clearance hole
from the same numbers. Nothing is spotted through anything and nothing depends on sighting down a
24 mm bore; the float in the plate's clearance hole is what buys that.

## FLAG: Check the rear plate's overhang before the side laminations go on

The sides were cut **1211**, where the drawing has them start {{SIDE_TONGUE_START}} forward of the
box's back edge. That offset exists for one reason: **the rear plate's end overhangs the box by
